CVPR 2026 was too large to summarize as a paper catalogue. This page is the index for a smaller report: the ideas that changed how I think about computer vision systems for geospatial AI and structured visual reasoning.

I went to Denver with Aerial2Poly very much in the foreground: an aerial image comes in, a sequence of polygon tokens comes out. The paper had received a strong accept to the MONTI workshop, and that made the framing feel concrete, but I was not there only to collect more remote-sensing papers. I wanted to widen my research taste: find the mechanism in each paper, strip it down to a simple example, and ask whether it could help structured geometry. It also left me with a quiet, concrete direction for a future Aerial2Poly upgrade, though the details are better kept private until the idea is ready.

One MONTI lesson stayed especially sharp: aerial imagery is not really a flat-image problem. It is a 3D, sensor, atmosphere, light, terrain, and physics problem projected into pixels. That made the geospatial papers easier to read with healthy skepticism: bigger foundation models matter, but so do scale, modality, acquisition, and the physical process that produced the image.

By the end, the trip felt less like a list of papers and more like a shift in taste. Diffusion made polygon generation look like repairing a damaged drawing. 3D reconstruction made intermediate geometry feel unavoidable. Tokenization papers made the output alphabet feel like a modeling choice. The report below is my attempt to keep that shift useful without turning it into a full diary.

<p class="cvpr-callout">The central question is: how should modern AI represent and generate structured geometry from visual evidence?</p>

The reading rule is deliberately simple. For each paper I ask: what is being represented, what operation changes it, what keeps the output valid, and what is the closest structured-geometry example?

I also wrote a separate teaching page that rebuilds the underlying deep learning ideas from simple examples. It starts from a `224 x 224` image, builds up attention and cross-attention, then connects tokenization, diffusion, flow, 3D geometry, VLM evidence acquisition, and verifier-style inference.

The selection rule is simple: a paper belongs in this report only if it teaches a mechanism that transfers outside its benchmark.

The two practical threads are:

- **Remote sensing and geospatial AI:** how should a model turn aerial imagery into structured geometry such as polygons, vectors, object boundaries, or scene-level representations?
- **Visual inspection and grounded evidence:** how can images, videos, aerial views, and location context become reliable signals about structure, condition, scale, and uncertainty?
